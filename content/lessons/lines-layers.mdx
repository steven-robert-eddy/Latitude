A photograph is a flat rectangle, but the eye doesn't look at it flatly —
it enters somewhere, travels, and lands somewhere. Lines and layers are the
two main tools for directing that travel on purpose instead of leaving it
to chance.

## Lines as attention routing

Any line in a frame — a road, a fence, a shoreline, a row of windows, the
edge where a wall meets a floor — pulls the eye along its length. A leading
line uses that pull deliberately, entering the frame (often from a bottom
corner) and traveling toward the actual subject. A line that leads
*nowhere* — off an edge, into empty space, away from anything worth
looking at — spends the same visual energy for no payoff, and can actively
work against you by pulling attention away from your subject instead of
toward it.

**Diagonals versus horizontals.** A horizontal line reads as calm, stable,
at rest — the horizon itself is the obvious example. A diagonal line reads
as energetic, unstable, in motion, because it implies a direction that
isn't simply left-right across a level plane. Neither is correct in
general; they suit different subjects. A line that could be shot as either,
depending on your angle, is worth trying both ways.

## Foreground, midground, background

A flat image can still suggest three-dimensional depth, and the most
reliable way to build that illusion is to give the eye something at three
different distances at once: something close (foreground), something at a
middle distance (midground), and something far (background). Each layer
anchors a different depth cue — relative size, overlap, atmospheric haze —
and stacking all three does more to suggest real depth than any single one
alone. A frame with only a background reads as flat no matter how
interesting that background is; the same scene with a foreground element
added suddenly has somewhere for the eye to start.

## How focal length changes the stack

This is where lines and layers meet the gear itself. A wide focal length
exaggerates the size difference between near and far objects, pushing them
apart — foreground looms large, background shrinks and recedes, and the
layers separate strongly. A long (telephoto) focal length does the
opposite: it compresses apparent distance, making near and far objects
look closer in size and closer together than they actually are. Neither
compression nor separation is better. Wide suits scenes where you want the
foreground to dominate and pull the viewer in. Long suits scenes — a
distant mountain behind a closer ridge, a stack of buildings down a street
— where you want the layers themselves to feel dense and stacked, almost
flattened together.

<FieldNote>
  To isolate the compression effect from simple magnification, shoot the
  same scene wide and long *from the same physical spot*, not by zooming
  to "fill the frame" differently each time. If you move your feet to
  compensate for focal length, you're changing subject distance too, which
  changes depth of field and perspective on top of compression — two
  variables moving at once instead of one.
</FieldNote>
