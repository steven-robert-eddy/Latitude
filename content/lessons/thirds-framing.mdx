Rule of thirds — divide the frame into a 3×3 grid, put your subject or the
horizon along one of the lines or near one of the intersections — is the
first compositional rule almost everyone learns, and the first one worth
learning to break. It works as a starting heuristic because it avoids the
single most common beginner default: dead-center placement done by
accident, with no thought behind it. But "not the exact center" is a low
bar, and treating the grid itself as the goal produces its own kind of
formula.

## Where thirds breaks

A strong, symmetrical subject — a face shot straight-on, a building with a
centered doorway, anything with its own bilateral symmetry — often wants to
be centered, because centering it respects the symmetry that makes it
interesting in the first place. Off-center placement on a symmetrical
subject can read as an unresolved accident rather than a choice, fighting
the subject instead of supporting it. The rule of thirds is a good default
for asymmetrical scenes and a bad one for symmetrical subjects. Knowing
which kind of subject you're looking at matters more than the grid.

## Balance and visual weight

Placement is really a question of visual weight — how much a given element
pulls the eye — and weight isn't the same as size. A small bright object
against a dark background can outweigh a large dull one. A single
person can balance an entire empty half of the frame if the empty half is
doing something (leading toward them, or giving them room to look or move
into). Off-center placement works when something is balancing the other
side of the frame, even if that something is empty space used
deliberately — negative space, which gets its own lesson shortly. Off-center
placement fails when the other side of the frame is just empty by neglect.

## Frames within frames

A doorway, a window, an archway, a gap between branches — any shape that
encloses part of the scene can be used as a frame around your actual
subject, adding a layer of depth and directing attention inward. It's a
placement decision at a level up from where the subject sits in the grid:
first you find or create the outer frame, then you place the subject
inside it.

## Edges: the thing beginners never check

Grid, symmetry, and balance all describe the center of interest. Edges
are the opposite end of the frame, and they're the thing most beginners
forget to look at entirely — a stray hand entering from the side, a pole
growing out of someone's head because it lined up with the background,
a bright distracting object sitting unnoticed in a corner. None of these
are visible if you're only looking at your subject. Checking the edges
of the frame, deliberately, every time, before you press the shutter, is
a habit independent of any compositional rule — and it's the cheapest
fix in this entire lesson, because it costs nothing but attention.

<FieldNote>
  Scan the four edges of your viewfinder or screen last, right before you
  shoot, as its own separate step — not as part of looking at your subject.
  It takes under a second once it's a habit, and it catches the mistakes
  that no amount of thirds-grid thinking will.
</FieldNote>

## Horizon placement is a decision about subject

Where you put the horizon in a landscape isn't a thirds-grid technicality —
it's a statement about what the photo is about. Horizon low, sky-heavy
frame: the photo is about the sky. Horizon high, ground-heavy frame: the
photo is about the land. Horizon dead-center: often a sign nothing was
decided, though it can work when both halves are genuinely equal
partners in the image (a still lake with a strong reflection, for
instance).

<CheckYourself question="You're photographing a lone tree in an otherwise empty field, and want the emptiness of the field to be part of the point. Where would you place the tree, and why not dead center?">
  Off-center, typically along a thirds line, with the empty field occupying
  the larger remaining portion of the frame. Dead-center framing would
  balance the tree against equal space on both sides, which reads as the
  tree being the entire subject rather than the tree's smallness against
  the field being the subject — the off-center placement is what lets the
  negative space actually register as space rather than as symmetric
  padding.
</CheckYourself>
