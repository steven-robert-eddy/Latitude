A light meter doesn't measure brightness the way you'd think. It measures
how much light is coming off the scene, then recommends settings that
would render the *average* of that light as middle grey — a fixed
reference point, not a judgment about what the scene actually looked like.
For an average scene — a typical mix of light and dark tones — that
assumption works fine and the meter gets it right without your help. It
fails predictably in two situations, and both are worth knowing by name.

## Where the meter breaks

**Snow, sand, a white wall, a bright sky filling most of the frame** — a
scene that's genuinely brighter than average. The meter still tries to drag
the average down to middle grey, so it underexposes. Your snow comes out
the color of pavement.

**A dark room, a black cat, a shadowed subject filling most of the frame**
— genuinely darker than average. The meter drags the average *up* to
middle grey, so it overexposes. Your black cat comes out grey.

Neither is a malfunction. The meter is doing exactly what it's built to do;
it just can't tell the difference between "this scene is dark" and "this
scene is well-lit but I'm pointed at something dark." That distinction is
yours to make.

## Exposure compensation is the fix

Exposure compensation tells the camera "take your reading, then shift it by
this many stops" — positive to brighten, negative to darken, layered on top
of whatever the meter already decided. For the snow scene, dialing in
positive compensation fights the meter's instinct to darken it back toward
grey. For the shadowed scene, negative compensation fights its instinct to
brighten. You're not overriding the meter so much as telling it what kind
of scene it's actually looking at.

<FieldNote>
  There's no universal number — how much compensation a scene needs depends
  on how far it sits from average, which is a judgment call that gets
  faster with repetition. Bright snow in full sun often wants +1 to +1⅔.
  A dark, low-key scene often wants −1 to −2. Chimp the histogram, adjust,
  reshoot. That loop is the actual skill.
</FieldNote>

## Reading a histogram

A histogram is a graph of how many pixels sit at each brightness level,
dark on the left to bright on the right. A spike jammed against the right
edge means clipped highlights — pixels so bright the sensor recorded no
detail there at all, just "maximum." A spike against the left edge means
clipped shadows, the same problem in the dark direction. Neither spike
touching an edge, roughly, is what you're aiming for in a scene you want to
hold detail across.

## Why this matters more on a Fuji JPEG than on RAW

A RAW file captures more dynamic range than gets displayed, so a highlight
that looks blown on the camera's screen sometimes has recoverable detail
still sitting in the file — pull the exposure down in software and it
comes back. A JPEG, including every recipe-rendered Fuji JPEG, has already
thrown that extra data away at the moment of capture. If you clip a
highlight in-camera on a JPEG, there is nothing left to recover; the
information doesn't exist anymore. "Expose for the highlights" — bias
your exposure toward protecting the brightest parts of the scene, even
at the cost of underexposing everything else — is a nice-to-have on RAW
and close to mandatory on JPEG.

<CheckYourself question="You're shooting a backlit subject against a bright sky, straight off the meter, no compensation. What's most likely to go wrong, and in which direction would you correct?">
  The meter reads the whole frame including the bright sky, drags the
  average toward middle grey, and underexposes your subject — often
  silhouetting it. You'd dial in positive exposure compensation to lift the
  subject, accepting that the sky may now clip in exchange for a subject
  that isn't a black shape.
</CheckYourself>

## What you're building

The assignment isn't really about getting five correct exposures. It's
about building a fast internal check — "is this scene brighter or darker
than average, and by how much" — that runs before you press the shutter,
not after you've already lost the highlight.
