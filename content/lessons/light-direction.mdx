Photography means "writing with light," and yet direction of light is the
thing most beginners never consciously choose — they choose a subject, then
photograph whatever light happens to be falling on it from wherever they
happen to be standing. Direction of light is one of the few variables you
can control just by moving your feet, which makes it the cheapest
compositional decision available to you at any given moment.

## What each direction does

**Front light** — the light source behind you, falling directly on the
front of your subject — is the light beginners default to, because it's
the light you get by pointing your back at the sun and shooting whatever's
in front of you. It's also the flattest light there is. Shadows fall
directly behind the subject, hidden from the camera, so the subject reads
as flat color and shape with very little sense of three-dimensional form.
Front light is forgiving and rarely fails outright, which is exactly why it
also rarely stands out.

**Side light** — the source roughly ninety degrees to your line of sight —
is the opposite. Shadows fall across the subject, visible to the camera,
and those shadows are what sculpt form. Texture that's invisible in front
light — skin, bark, fabric weave, brick — suddenly reads as texture, because
texture *is* small-scale shadow, and side light is what reveals small
shadows. This is the light direction most likely to make an ordinary
subject look three-dimensional.

**Back light** — the source behind the subject, facing the camera — is the
hardest to expose and the easiest to get wrong by accident. Handled
deliberately, it produces rim light (a bright edge outlining the subject,
separating it from the background) or a silhouette (the subject rendered as
pure dark shape against a bright background). Handled by accident, it
produces the same underexposed, flat subject anyone gets from pointing a
camera at the sun without a plan.

## Reading the light before you raise the camera

The skill this lesson is actually teaching is a habit, not a fact: before
you frame anything, notice where the light is coming from relative to
where you're standing, and treat that as a variable you can change by
moving. Front-lit is the default state of doing nothing. Side- and
back-light both require you to walk somewhere.

<FieldNote>
  On a clear day, your shadow tells you the light direction instantly — it
  points away from the source. If your shadow stretches out in front of
  you, you're front-lighting whatever's ahead of you. Walk around your
  subject until your shadow points roughly toward the camera position
  instead, and you're now side- or back-lighting it.
</FieldNote>

## Metering for backlight

A backlit subject against a bright sky is exactly the meter failure from
the previous lesson: the meter reads the bright background, drags the
average down, and your subject goes dark or silhouettes. That's not always
wrong — a deliberate silhouette is a real creative choice — but it should
be a choice. If you want detail in the subject rather than a silhouette,
you need positive exposure compensation, sometimes a lot of it, and you
need to accept that the background may clip in exchange.

<CheckYourself question="You want a silhouette, not a detailed subject, against a backlit sky. What do you do with exposure compensation, if anything?">
  Nothing, or even negative compensation. A silhouette is what the meter's
  failure mode produces by default when you don't correct it — the subject
  going dark against a bright background is the effect you want here, so
  there's no failure to fix.
</CheckYourself>
