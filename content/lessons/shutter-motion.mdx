Aperture and ISO both ultimately serve exposure. Shutter speed does that
too, but it's the one dial with a second job: it's a decision about time.
Every other setting asks "how much light." Shutter speed additionally asks
"how much of the subject's motion do you want visible in a still image."

## Freezing versus describing

A fast enough shutter stops motion so completely that a swinging bat, a
dog mid-jump, or a wave breaking looks like it was never moving at all —
information the eye never actually sees, because human vision doesn't
have a shutter. A slow shutter does the opposite: it lets motion write
itself across the frame as blur, which is closer to how the moment
actually felt. Neither is more correct. Freezing a hummingbird's wings
tells you what they look like frozen — informative, occasionally more
false to the moment than a bit of blur would have been. There's no
technically-right shutter speed for a moving subject, only the one that
matches what you're trying to say about it.

## The reciprocal rule is a floor, not a target

For a subject that isn't moving but your hands are — handheld shake, not
subject motion — there's a rough rule: don't shoot slower than 1 over your
focal length in 35mm-equivalent terms. A 50mm lens wants at least 1/50,
a 200mm lens wants at least 1/200. Camera and lens stabilization push that
floor lower, sometimes by several stops. But it's a floor, not a target.
Shooting exactly at the reciprocal-rule speed is shooting at the edge of
what's handholdable, and "the edge of handholdable" is not a place to
plan a keeper. If you don't need the shutter speed for a creative reason,
give yourself margin above the floor.

<FieldNote>
  The reciprocal rule is about *your* stability, not the subject's motion.
  A person standing still needs 1/50 at 50mm to survive your hands
  shaking. A person running needs a much faster shutter than that,
  regardless of how steady you are, or their motion blurs regardless of
  what your hands do.
</FieldNote>

## Panning: matching your motion to theirs

A pan asks you to do something that feels backwards at first: move the
camera to keep a moving subject in the same spot in the frame throughout
the exposure. Done well, the subject stays relatively sharp while
everything static in the scene — the background, the ground — streaks
into motion lines. It's the shutter-speed lesson turned into a physical
skill: you're using a moderately slow shutter (often 1/30 to 1/125,
subject-dependent) not to blur the subject but to blur everything *except*
the subject, by matching your camera's angular motion to theirs.

<CheckYourself question="Why might a 1/500 shutter fail to freeze a subject that a 1/250 shutter froze cleanly in a different shot?">
  Shutter speed alone doesn't determine whether motion is frozen — the
  *rate* of the subject's apparent motion across the frame matters too. A
  subject moving directly toward or away from the camera has less apparent
  motion than the same subject moving laterally across the frame at the
  same real-world speed, and a subject closer to the lens or shot at a
  longer focal length covers more of the frame per second than the same
  motion farther away or at a wider focal length. 1/500 on a fast lateral
  subject up close can blur more than 1/250 on a subject moving toward the
  camera.
</CheckYourself>

## What the pan attempt is actually for

You are very likely to fail at this the first time, and that's the point
of asking for a genuine attempt rather than a successful one. Panning is a
timing and tracking skill, not a settings choice — the shutter speed gets
you in the right range, but the keeper rate comes from repetition. Log
what you used and what happened. The failure is data for next time.
