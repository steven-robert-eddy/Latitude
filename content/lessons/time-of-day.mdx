The sun's angle changes more than brightness over the course of a day — it
changes the physical path light travels through the atmosphere, and that
changes color, softness, and direction all at once. Low sun near sunrise or
sunset passes through far more atmosphere than sun directly overhead,
scattering out blue wavelengths and leaving the warm end of the spectrum —
that's the actual mechanism behind "golden hour," not a marketing term for
"pretty light." The same low angle means shadows fall long and soft-edged,
and because the light source sits near the horizon, it's more likely to hit
subjects from the side rather than straight down — which, per the previous
lesson, is the direction that reveals form and texture.

## Why midday is hard, not just less flattering

At midday the sun sits nearly overhead, and light traveling that short a
path through the atmosphere stays close to full-spectrum white — harsher,
more contrasty, less forgiving. Shadows fall short and hard-edged, straight
down, which on a face means dark sockets under the brow and nose. It's not
that midday light is worse light; it's that it demands more of you. Soft
golden-hour light is close to failure-proof. Hard midday light will
readily produce a bad photo if you don't have a plan for it. Notice that
none of this is about exposure settings — you could shoot both sessions at
identical aperture, shutter, and ISO and still come home with two entirely
different kinds of photograph. Time of day changes the light itself, not
just how much of it there is.

## What midday is actually for

Hard light isn't a light to avoid, it's a light with a different job. It
produces deep, saturated color rather than the pastel wash of golden hour.
It produces graphic, high-contrast shadows you can use as compositional
shapes in their own right — something the next lesson goes into directly.
It suits architecture, where crisp hard-edged shadows read as strong
geometry rather than a problem to solve. The failure mode isn't shooting at
midday; it's shooting at midday with a golden-hour mindset and being
disappointed when flat, soft light doesn't show up.

<FieldNote>
  Open shade — the shadow of a building, a tree canopy, anything that
  blocks direct sun without fully enclosing the subject — is the midday
  escape hatch. It trades the sun's hard direct light for soft, indirect
  skylight, but that skylight itself skews blue, especially under a clear
  sky, which is why faces shot in open shade often need a slight warm
  correction to look natural.
</FieldNote>

## Blue hour, briefly

After sunset (or before sunrise), there's a window — blue hour — where the
sun is below the horizon but the sky still glows, now skewed cool rather
than warm. It's shorter and less forgiving than golden hour, exposures run
long, and it rewards scenes with their own light sources (city lights,
windows, streetlamps) that can balance against the darkening sky. Worth
knowing exists; not the subject of this assignment.

<CheckYourself question="You have exactly one hour to shoot a portrait outdoors and can pick any time of day. Golden hour isn't available. What's your next-best option and why?">
  Open shade at any time of day — it gives you the soft, low-contrast
  quality of golden hour (via indirect skylight rather than direct sun)
  without needing the sun to be low. Correct for the blue cast in editing
  or via white balance, and it holds up close to as well as the golden-hour
  session for a portrait specifically.
</CheckYourself>
