A shadow isn't an absence of information, even though it's easy to treat it
that way — the part of the frame with nothing interesting in it, tolerated
rather than used. Flip that assumption and shadows become one of the more
reliable compositional tools available, because unlike most compositional
elements, they cost you nothing to place: move a few feet and the shadow
moves with you.

## Shape

A shadow has its own outline, separate from the object casting it, and that
outline is often more graphic and simplified than the object itself — a
tangle of bare branches can cast a shadow that reads as a clean web of
lines on a plain wall. Once you start looking for shadow shapes
independently of their source, you start finding compositions that don't
exist if you only look at the lit side of the scene.

## Edge quality: hard versus soft

A shadow's edge tells you about the light source that made it. Direct,
undiffused light from a small or distant source — direct sun on a clear
day being the everyday example — casts a hard-edged shadow: a crisp line
between lit and unlit. An overcast sky, open shade, or any large diffused
light source casts a soft-edged shadow that gradates rather than cuts.
Neither is better; they're different tools. Hard shadows read as graphic
and high-contrast — useful for the shape-based compositions above. Soft
shadows read as gentle, more true to how the previous lesson's open-shade
option renders a face.

<FieldNote>
  You can predict edge quality without seeing the shadow yet, just by
  looking at the light source itself: small or distant source, hard edge;
  large or close source, soft edge. That's the same principle a studio
  photographer uses when they move a light closer to soften it — proximity
  makes a source effectively larger relative to the subject.
</FieldNote>

## Contrast ratio, informally

The difference in brightness between a scene's lit areas and its shadow
areas is its contrast ratio. High contrast — bright highlights, deep dark
shadows, little in between — tends to read as dramatic and graphic. Low
contrast reads as calm, sometimes flat. You don't need to measure this
formally; you need to notice it, because it's the thing your camera's meter
struggles with most (this is the same highlight/shadow problem from the
metering lesson, now framed as a compositional choice rather than a
technical failure to fix).

## Using shadow to hide

The most practical use of shadow has nothing to do with drama: it's
concealment. A cluttered background, a stray object, an ugly edge of frame
— any of these can sit inside a shadow and simply disappear, because deep
shadow reads as near-black regardless of what's actually in it. This is
compositional simplification for free, without moving your subject or
cropping anything out. Look for the messy part of a scene, then look for
whether it happens to already be sitting in shade — or whether you could
reposition so that it is.

<CheckYourself question="A scene you want to shoot has a distracting trash can in the background, in full sun. You can't move it and can't crop it out of your composition. What's a shadow-based fix?">
  Reposition so the trash can falls into an existing shadow — from a
  building, a tree, an awning — or wait until the sun's angle puts it in
  shade. A shadow, even one that isn't your subject's own, can absorb
  clutter that would otherwise fight for attention in bright, evenly lit
  detail.
</CheckYourself>

## What removing the shadow would prove

The success criteria ask you to imagine the frame without its shadow. If
the photo would still basically work — same subject, same story, minus a
dark patch — the shadow wasn't structural, it was decoration. This
assignment is asking for the frames where that removal test actually fails.
