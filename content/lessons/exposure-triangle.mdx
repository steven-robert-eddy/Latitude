Three dials control how much light reaches the sensor: aperture, shutter
speed, and ISO. Picture them as three taps filling the same bucket. Open one
tap wider and the bucket fills faster — so you can close another tap and
still hit the same water level. That's the entire exposure triangle. Everything
else is detail.

## Stops: the one unit that matters

A **stop** is a doubling or a halving. One stop more light, one stop less
light — the direction is in the word. It doesn't matter which dial produces
it:

- One stop of aperture is <Stop from="f/2.8" to="f/4" /> — half the light,
  because the area of the opening halves.
- One stop of shutter speed is 1/500 to 1/250 — twice as long, so twice the
  light.
- One stop of ISO is 200 to 400 — the sensor amplifies the signal twice as
  much.

Because a stop means the same thing everywhere, you can trade them freely.
Close the aperture one stop, open the shutter one stop, and the exposure is
identical — you've just changed *how* you got there, not *how much* light
you got.

## Equivalent exposures

This is why the three example settings in the assignment brief all produce
the same brightness. Walk it through:

- f/2.8 → f/5.6 is two stops less light through the aperture.
- 1/500 → 1/125 is two stops more time for light to hit the sensor.

They cancel. Same for the second pair. Three different-looking combinations
of numbers, one identical exposure. A camera's meter doesn't know or care
which combination you pick — it only cares about the total.

<FieldNote>
  If you're not sure two settings are a stop apart, halve or double a number
  and see if it lands close to the other. f/4 doubled in area is roughly
  f/5.6, not f/8 — apertures don't move in round numbers because they're
  tracking area, not diameter. Shutter speeds and ISO do move in round
  numbers, which is part of why aperture feels like the confusing one at
  first.
</FieldNote>

## Why the camera's "correct" exposure is an opinion

Point a meter at a scene and it will suggest a combination of the three
settings that lands on a target brightness — historically, middle grey. But
"correct" here means "matches a brightness convention," not "the only valid
setting." The meter has no idea whether you want the background blurred into
nothing or sharp enough to read a street sign four blocks away. It has no
idea whether your subject is about to run or intends to hold still for the
next ten minutes. Those are your decisions, and they're exactly what
aperture and shutter speed actually control beyond exposure — depth of field
and motion. ISO is the one dial that's mostly just a cost: raising it buys
you more flexibility in the other two at the price of noise.

So "what's the correct exposure" is the wrong question to carry into a
shoot. The right one is: given the brightness I need, which dial do I want
to spend on depth of field, which on freezing or blurring motion, and how
much noise am I willing to accept from ISO to get both? The exposure
triangle is the arithmetic that makes that trade-off possible. Everything
from here — depth of field, motion, metering — is about learning to make
that trade-off on purpose instead of by accident.

<CheckYourself question="You're at f/4, 1/250, ISO 400, and the meter says you're one stop under. Name two different single-dial changes that would fix it.">
  Open the aperture to f/2.8 (one more stop of light), **or** slow the
  shutter to 1/125 (one more stop of light), **or** raise the ISO to 800
  (one more stop of gain). Any one of the three closes the gap on its own —
  which one you'd actually reach for depends on what you're willing to give
  up: depth of field, motion freeze, or noise.
</CheckYourself>

## What the assignment is actually testing

Three frames, same brightness, different routes. If you nail the exposure
match but can't see a difference in depth of field between the widest and
narrowest aperture, look again at your framing — the effect is real but it's
easy to shoot past it if the background is already far away or already
blurry for other reasons. The point isn't the arithmetic. It's building the
reflex of seeing "one stop" as a single, portable unit you can move between
three different dials without thinking twice.
