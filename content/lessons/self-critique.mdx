You already have a reaction to your own photos — you like some, you're
indifferent to others, a few embarrass you. That reaction is fast,
automatic, and not entirely trustworthy, because it's contaminated by
things that have nothing to do with the photograph: you remember how the
light felt on your skin that day, you remember the effort it took to get
the shot, you remember who you were with. None of that is visible in the
image itself, but all of it leaks into how you feel about it. Gut reaction
measures the photo plus the memory of taking it. A framework, applied
deliberately, tries to measure the photo alone.

## Why record both, not just the framework

It would be simpler to skip the gut pass and go straight to structured
scoring. The reason not to: the *gap* between gut and framework is where
the actual information lives. A photo you loved on instinct that scores
poorly on the framework is telling you something about what you're
currently drawn to that isn't yet showing up as technical or compositional
strength — worth knowing, not worth dismissing as "the framework must be
wrong." A photo you were indifferent to that scores well is telling you
that your instincts haven't caught up to what you're actually now capable
of. Neither gap is a problem to fix. Both are signal.

## The five axes

**Focus** — is the intended subject sharp where it needs to be, and is
anything unintentionally soft that shouldn't be? A technically-simple
question, but it's the axis most likely to be quietly ignored when a photo
is otherwise appealing.

**Exposure** — does the image hold detail where you wanted it, informed by
everything in this curriculum's first block? A well-exposed photo of a dull
subject and a poorly-exposed photo of an interesting one are different
kinds of failure, and the framework wants you to name which one you're
looking at.

**Composition** — placement, lines, layers, negative space: is the frame
doing deliberate work, or did it just happen to contain the subject?

**Light** — direction, quality, time of day: is the light doing something
for the photograph, or is it simply present?

**Subject** — independent of all the above craft questions: is there
actually something here worth a viewer's attention? A technically flawless
photo of nothing in particular still fails this axis.

Score each axis honestly and separately — a weak exposure shouldn't drag
down your composition score just because they're both low on the same
photo. They're different questions about the same image.

<FieldNote>
  Score focus and exposure first, before composition and light. They're
  closer to binary — sharp or not, held or clipped — and scoring the more
  judgment-heavy axes right after them tends to anchor your composition and
  light scores to how technically clean the frame already is, which is
  exactly the contamination this framework exists to avoid.
</FieldNote>

## Separating "I remember this" from "this is good"

The clearest version of the trap this lesson names: a photo from a trip you
loved, of a moment that mattered to you, that is — looked at coldly — soft,
oddly framed, badly lit. The gut score will be high. That's not a mistake
to correct; it's real information about what the photo means to you. The
framework score, done honestly, will likely be lower, and that's also real
information, about the photograph as an object separate from your memory
of taking it. Holding both at once, without collapsing one into the other,
is the actual skill.

<CheckYourself question="You gut-rated a photo 5/5 and it scores 2/5 on the framework — weak composition, harsh midday light, otherwise fine. What should you do with the gut rating?">
  Nothing — don't revise it. The gut rating already happened and recording
  it honestly is the point of the exercise; going back to adjust it toward
  the framework score defeats the purpose, which is to see the *gap*
  between the two, not to make them agree. The interesting question is why
  the gap exists for this particular photo, not which number is "right."
</CheckYourself>

## Why the gut ratings lock

Once you start the framework pass, your gut ratings for that batch are
locked — not adjustable, even if a framework score makes you want to
second-guess an earlier gut call. This isn't a technicality. If gut ratings
could be revised after seeing the framework, every divergence would quietly
disappear the moment you noticed it, and the divergence is the entire
point of doing two passes instead of one.

## Where this goes next

This assignment is the on-ramp to the culling trainer: the same blind-gut
pass and five-axis framework, formalized, run across your whole photo
library rather than one batch of twenty. What you're building here — the
habit of separating instinct from analysis, and the vocabulary of five
specific axes instead of a vague "I like it" — is the skill the rest of
that tool assumes you already have.
