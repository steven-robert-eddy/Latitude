f/2.8. f/5.6. f/16. The numbers look arbitrary until you know what they
actually measure: not a size, but a ratio. f-number is focal length divided
by the diameter of the aperture opening. f/2.8 on a 50mm lens means an
opening about 18mm across; f/2.8 on a 200mm lens means an opening about
71mm across. Different physical sizes, identical exposure, because the
ratio — and therefore the amount of light relative to the image — is the
same. That's the entire point of the f-number system: it lets "f/2.8" mean
the same thing on every lens you own.

## Depth of field is a side effect, not a setting

Depth of field — how much of the scene in front of and behind your focus
point looks acceptably sharp — isn't controlled by aperture alone. It's a
function of three things at once: aperture, subject distance, and focal
length. Widen the aperture and depth of field shrinks. Move closer to your
subject and it shrinks again, independent of aperture. Zoom in and it
shrinks a third time. All three stack, which is why a close-up portrait at
f/4 can have less in focus than a landscape at f/1.4 shot from thirty feet
away.

<FieldNote>
  The X-T20's sensor is APS-C — about 1.5x smaller than full frame. At a
  given f-number and framing, that gives slightly *more* depth of field than
  the same shot on a full-frame body. It's a small effect next to distance
  and focal length, but it's why "f/1.4 on my phone" and "f/1.4 on a
  full-frame camera" don't blur backgrounds the same amount.
</FieldNote>

## Wide open is not free

It's tempting to treat "shoot wide open" as a default — more blur reads as
more skill, and a wider aperture also means more light, which means a
faster shutter or lower ISO for free. But lenses are usually softest at
their widest aperture. Optical designs make compromises at the edges of
their range, and the edge most beginners live at is wide open. Stop down
two or three stops from maximum and most lenses noticeably sharpen,
especially in the corners — then, past about f/11 on most lenses, a
different effect called diffraction starts softening the image again as the
opening gets small enough to bend light around its edges. There's a sweet
spot in the middle, not at either end.

None of this means wide open is wrong. A shallow depth of field that
isolates a subject from a busy background is a real creative choice, not a
mistake. The point is that "wide open" and "sharpest" are different
questions, and conflating them is the beginner tell this lesson is named
after.

<CheckYourself question="Your 35mm lens is at f/2.8, focused on someone's face from six feet away. Name two changes — one aperture-related, one not — that would each increase depth of field on their own.">
  Aperture: stop down, e.g. to f/5.6 or f/8. Not aperture: step back and
  increase your distance to the subject (re-framing with a longer focal
  length to compensate, if you want to keep the same composition — which
  changes depth of field again, in the other direction, which is the whole
  reason this stuff takes practice to feel automatic).
</CheckYourself>

## What to look for in your own ladder

Line the frames up side by side once you're done shooting. The background
should go from readable to smooth as you widen; the subject should stay
sharp throughout unless your focus drifted. If the *sharpest* frame on your
actual subject isn't the widest aperture in the set, that's not a mistake —
that's the lesson.
