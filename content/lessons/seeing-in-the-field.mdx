Every lesson in this curriculum so far has isolated one variable —
aperture, shutter, light direction, placement — because that's how skills
get learned: one at a time, deliberately, in a controlled comparison.
Actual shooting doesn't work that way. In the field, all of it runs at
once, and the photographers who look like they're not thinking about any
of it are usually running through a sequence so practiced it's become
unconscious. This lesson tries to make that sequence conscious, at least
once, so it has a chance to become a habit rather than staying a list.

## The sequence

Roughly, in order: **notice the light** — direction, quality, whether it's
doing anything worth using. **Find a subject** — not necessarily the first
thing you saw, but the thing that light is actually flattering. **Choose a
position** — where you stand determines light direction relative to you,
background, and layering, all at once, per the last several lessons.
**Set exposure** — the mechanical layer, now informed by everything above
it. **Check the edges** — the one-second habit from the placement lesson,
run every time, not just when you remember. **Shoot. Then adjust** — look
at what you got, and let it inform the next frame rather than treating each
shot as independent.

None of these steps are new information at this point in the curriculum.
What's new is running them in sequence, repeatedly, on one subject, instead
of running one step per subject before moving on.

## Working a scene instead of taking a photo and leaving

The habit this assignment is built to break is the single-frame-and-move-on
pattern: arrive, take a shot, leave. Working a scene means treating your
first frame as a starting point, not a conclusion — then asking what
changes if you move three feet left, if you crouch, if you wait two
minutes for someone to walk into frame or the light to shift, if you switch
from wide to long. Fifteen frames of genuine variation on one subject will
almost always beat fifteen frames of fifteen different subjects, because
depth of exploration on one scene teaches you more than breadth across
many — you're forced to actually exhaust the first four or five obvious
compositions before anything more interesting shows up.

<FieldNote>
  When you feel like you've run out of ideas for a scene — usually somewhere
  around frame five or six — that's the actual starting line, not a sign to
  move on. The obvious compositions get shot first because they're obvious.
  What comes after you've exhausted them is where working a scene actually
  pays off.
</FieldNote>

## Pre-visualization

Experienced photographers often describe seeing the finished photograph
before they raise the camera — not literally, but as a strong enough sense
of what a given position and light and moment will produce that the camera
becomes a tool for confirming a decision rather than a tool for discovering
one. You won't have this yet, and that's fine; it's built by exactly the
kind of repetition this assignment asks for; forty-five minutes on one
scene, dozens of times over, eventually turns the conscious sequence above
into something closer to instinct.

## When a scene is actually done

There's no fixed rule, but a reasonable signal: you've run the sequence
several times, tried genuinely different positions and framings rather
than minor variations, and new attempts are starting to repeat earlier
ones rather than add anything. That's different from simply getting bored
or tired, which happens well before a scene is actually exhausted — part
of what this assignment is training is the patience to push past the point
where leaving starts to feel appealing.

<CheckYourself question="You've shot 20 frames of a scene in 10 minutes and feel done. Is that a sign the scene is exhausted, or a sign you left too early?">
  Almost certainly too early. Twenty frames in ten minutes is a pace that
  suggests you're clicking through minor variations rather than genuinely
  changing position, focal length, and waiting for the scene to shift —
  working a scene properly takes time specifically because repositioning,
  trying a different focal length, and waiting for better light or a
  better moment all take longer than pressing the shutter does.
</CheckYourself>

## What your top three should show

If your best three frames are your first three, you likely didn't push
past the obvious compositions — which is the most common way this
assignment goes wrong, and worth honestly checking for rather than
explaining away.
